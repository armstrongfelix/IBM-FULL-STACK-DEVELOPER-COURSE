Expert Viewpoints: A Typical Interview Cycle
1. Example of a Two-Interview Structure
While interview processes vary heavily by company, one expert outlined their preferred two-round approach for assessing developers:

First Interview (30 Minutes - Technical):

Attendees: The candidate, the hiring manager, and a senior developer.

Focus: Strictly technical aspects and previous experiences. Interviewers ask about past projects and review code (e.g., on GitHub).

Format: Relevant to the job and the candidate's existing experience. This expert specifically avoids overly algorithmic questions or unrelated "brain teasers" (e.g., "how many golf balls are in New York City?").

Second Interview (1 Hour - Soft Skills):

Attendees: The candidate, the hiring manager, and the second-line manager.

Focus: Soft skills, communication, and ambitions.

Purpose: At this point, the managers usually have a good idea of the candidate's capabilities. This round is primarily used to sell the candidate on the team's mission and culture, and for the candidate to learn more about the company.

Note on Industry Variations: Large corporations ("Big Tech") often use much longer, grueling multi-round processes (sometimes up to 5 hours). If you want to work at those specific companies, you must be prepared to endure that specific style of process.

2. The Purpose of the Interview Process
There is no single "typical" interview process because companies look for different skills and individuals.

Types of Assessments: You may encounter language checks, coding assessments, competency-based interviews, and behavioral assessments.

The Right Mindset: A good selection process is not designed to put hurdles in front of you or trick you. It is designed to get a "full view" of you—understanding both your strengths and your areas for development so the company can envision what your training program would look like. View it as an opportunity to show how good you are, not a trap to expose your flaws.

3. Preparation and Research Tips
Thorough preparation is the number one tip for an upcoming interview.

Research the Job Post: Start by extracting as much information as possible directly from the job listing.

Research the Interviewers: If you know who is interviewing you, look them up to understand what they are passionate about. Example: If an interviewer is obsessed with Python, take time to brush up on Python.

Research the Company: Understand the team, the mission statement, the CEO, and the overall work environment.

Prepare Questions: Being prepared ensures you have strong questions ready when the interviewers inevitably ask, "Do you have any questions for us?"

The Major Red Flag: A candidate who knows nothing about the company and barely skimmed the job posting sends a massive red flag. While not strictly a deal-breaker, it makes a very poor first impression.

4. Making a Good First Impression (Nerves and Body Language)
Interviews are largely about first impressions, meaning your physical presence and communication style matter immensely.

Manage Distracting Habits: Nervous habits (like tapping your foot) can be highly distracting to the interviewer. Find ways to calm your nerves: sit on your hands, cross them, or hold a drink or a piece of paper. Remember that a good interviewer will recognize you are nervous and try to help you.

Vocal Tone and Gestures: Be mindful of your tone of voice and what you do with your hands (avoid flinging them around excessively if you are a hand-talker).

Body Language: Ensure your posture signals interest. Sit forward and appear engaged, and try to mirror the body language and behaviors of the person interviewing you, rather than sitting back and slouching.
