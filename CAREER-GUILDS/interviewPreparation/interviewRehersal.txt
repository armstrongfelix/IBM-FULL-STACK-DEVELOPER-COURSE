Interview Rehearsal
1. The Importance of Rehearsal
Start Early: You do not need to have an interview scheduled to begin preparing. Doing the groundwork early ensures you are ready when an opportunity arises.

Bridge the Presentation Gap: There is a major difference between simply knowing your background and being able to smoothly present it verbally. Rehearsing your delivery bridges this gap.

Build Confidence: Doing the work to rehearse acts as a secret weapon. Because many candidates neglect this step, rehearsing gives you a distinct advantage in confidence and poise, helping you stand out.

Develop Soft Skills: Delivering information smoothly is a soft skill that you will utilize in almost any professional role, not just during interviews.

2. The Elevator Pitch
An elevator pitch is a brief introduction of yourself and your career goals. It is highly useful for networking and for answering the standard "tell us about yourself" interview prompt.

Structure: It should be about three or four sentences covering your name and title, your recent experience, and exactly what you are looking for in a new job.

Example from Transcript: "My name is Angela Menendez and I’m a data scientist. For the past two years, I’ve worked in an entry-level role helping derive business insights from data using established models at an e-commerce company. I would eventually like to move into designing machine learning models, and I’m looking for a mid-level position with opportunities to work toward that goal."

Execution: Compose your pitch, memorize it, and practice saying it repeatedly until it flows naturally.

3. Preparing for Common Questions
Anticipate Standard Inquiries: Every interview has specific questions, but many rely on common prompts, such as:  

Why did you choose this field, and why are you interested in this job?  

Tell us about a time you solved a problem on a project and how you did it.  

Where do you see yourself in five years?  

Draft Original Answers: Research these common questions on websites and take the time to think through original responses.  

Use Your Resume: Review your own resume to trigger memories of the specific skills you have used and the distinct tasks you have accomplished.  

4. Preparing Questions for the Interviewer
Interviewing is a two-way street. Do not rely on coming up with questions in the moment; prepare them ahead of time.

Company-Specific Inquiries: Use the notes you gathered while researching the company to customize your questions. Ask about their products, company structure, or recent news releases.

Role and Culture Inquiries: Ask questions that align with your career plans and preferred work style. Examples include asking whether the team works closely together or independently, or inquiring about the typical promotion path from the role.

Verbal Practice: Practice asking these questions aloud. Becoming comfortable with the wording makes it easier to genuinely listen to the answers and respond naturally.

5. Effective Rehearsal Techniques
Read Aloud and Paraphrase: Spend time reading your prepared answers and questions aloud, then try paraphrasing them. Aside from the elevator pitch, you do not need to memorize your answers word-for-word; just practice until you are comfortable speaking from your notes.

Mirror Work: Watch yourself in a mirror to identify any nervous physical habits and to practice maintaining eye contact.

Self-Recording: Record yourself (audio or video) to evaluate how you sound and appear to others.

Conduct Mock Interviews: Once you feel comfortable, enlist a friend to act as the interviewer.

Simulate Stress: Practicing verbal answers with another person helps you get used to thinking and speaking under the pressure of a real interview.

Spontaneous Practice: Allow your friend to ask questions you have not researched to practice coming up with answers quickly on your feet.

Ask Your Questions: Use the mock interview to practice asking the questions you prepared for the employer.
